# Related work

This note places the project against five lines of prior work. Each paragraph says what the line of work shows, what it reports as not working, and how this project's design and results differ. Numbers for this project are quoted as they stand in the reports under `docs/reports/`, with the section given; references are numbered at the end, and a note on how each was checked follows the list.

## 1. Map and spatial benchmarks for vision-language models

Map reasoning in foundation models is mostly measured by benchmarks. MapEval [1] poses 700 multiple-choice questions over 180 cities in three modes (textual context, tool calls against a map API, map screenshots); none of 30 models exceeds 67 %, all trail humans by more than 20 points, and distances, directions and route planning are named as the weak spots. MapQA [2] generates 3,154 question–answer pairs from OpenStreetMap with SQL templates and attaches the geometries of the referenced places; it is a text benchmark, and its finding is that LLMs handle one-hop questions but not multi-hop ones. MapBench [5] asks for navigation instructions between landmarks on 100 map images and reports that it "significantly challenges" current models. On the training side, MapTrace [6] generates synthetic map images with pixel-exact path labels (23k paths on 4k maps) and raises route-tracing success on MapBench by up to 6.4 points after fine-tuning; RewardMap [3] trains Qwen2.5-VL on transit-map questions with a multi-stage reinforcement-learning curriculum and partial-credit rewards; SpatialThinker [4] uses dense spatial rewards and online RL on 7k synthetic samples. Two things are common to this line. The input is a picture of a map (a screenshot, a rendered tile, a transit diagram), and the answer is text: an option, a name, a coordinate list. The model under test is a large pretrained network whose internal computation is not examined.

This project is the controlled counterpart. The input is not a rendered map but a stack of spatially aligned data planes at one metre per pixel (buildings, surfaces, noise, places, sunlit hours), every target is computed exactly from those planes, and the model is small enough (4.8–8.9 M parameters) to be probed head by head. The cost is breadth: one city, a few task families, no language. What the Qwen track adds to the benchmark picture is a measurement on a task with an exact geometric answer and a single scoring rule. Qwen3-VL [7] zero-shot solves 2.5 % (2B) and 24.5 % (4B) of the first 200 De Pijp validation routes under the polyline rule, against a straight-line floor of 27.6 %; the 2B model mostly misplaces the endpoints and the 4B model draws straight lines through buildings on about half of its routes (Track A report §2.1–2.2; Comparison table §2). LoRA fine-tuning [8] of the 2B model on 19.3k routes reaches 49.6 % of 500 routes, 28 % on routes of six or more fronts, and the remaining failure is still geometric: on 36 % of routes the polyline crosses a building (Track A report §3.3–3.4). That is consistent with the benchmark finding that route planning is weak, and it locates the weakness more precisely: after fine-tuning the model grounds the endpoints but does not plan around obstacles.

## 2. Learned planning and search in neural networks

**Planning modules built into the network.** Value Iteration Networks [9] embed a differentiable approximation of value iteration as a recurrent convolution and train it end to end on grid path planning, reporting better generalisation to unseen domains than reactive policies. Gated Path Planning Networks [10] recast VIN as a recurrent convolutional network and report that VIN is unstable to train and sensitive to seeds and hyperparameters; a gated recurrent update fixes much of that across maze types and sizes. What works here is a planning recurrence with a fixed, local structure; what does not is the optimisation of the raw VIN form. Our network has no built-in planning operator: the recurrence is a plain transformer applied once per pass, and the algorithm is taught through targets, not through architecture.

**Neural algorithmic reasoning.** Veličković and Blundell [11] argue for networks that execute classical algorithms, and the CLRS benchmark [12] supplies "hints", the intermediate states of each algorithm, and tests at four times the training problem size (64 against 16) to measure out-of-distribution generalisation. The idea we take is the hint: supervise every intermediate state, not only the answer. Our wave is such a hint sequence in raster form, one front per 40 m of walking, then one step of the way back per pass (C5 report §1–2). The difference is the substrate: CLRS works on graphs with node and edge features; we run on a real city raster with several layers, and the state is a dense image that the network must both read and redraw.

**Recurrent depth and extrapolation.** Deep Thinking networks [13] apply one recurrent block many times and show that networks trained on small mazes (and prefix sums, chess puzzles) solve larger ones by iterating longer. The follow-up [14] reports that this breaks down on hard instances when many iterations are applied ("overthinking") and fixes it with a recall connection that re-injects the problem at every iteration and a progressive loss that discourages iteration-count-specific behaviour; trained on 9 × 9 mazes, its models solve 74 % of 201 × 201 test mazes. Our loop has the recall connection by construction, since the map planes are re-fed at every pass. It differs in three ways: the state passed between iterations is an explicit, human-readable drawing rather than a latent feature map; the number of passes is decided by a state token the network emits (SEARCH, BACK, DRAW, DONE) rather than fixed or chosen by a confidence rule; and the network is a global-attention transformer, not a convolution with a local receptive field. What we have not shown is the Deep Thinking result itself. Our networks were trained on routes of all lengths in their window; the train-short, test-long experiment planned in `docs/plans/C5_PLAN.md` (R5d) was not run. The 256 m network moved unchanged to a 512 m window solves 2.6 %, because it has never seen positions beyond its patch grid; continued at 512 m and given one round of natural noise it solves 42 %, falling to 4 % on routes of 13 or more fronts (C5 report §4.7).

**Pathfinding neural cellular automata.** Earle et al. [15] show that neural cellular automata, iterated local update rules whose input and output have the same shape, can implement breadth-first search by hand and can be trained to compute maze diameters, with out-of-distribution performance improved by adversarially mutated training mazes. Our search is closest to this in form: input and output have the same shape, and the computation taught is a breadth-first wave. The difference is locality. An NCA's rule is local by construction, which is what lets it carry over to new maze layouts; our transformer attends globally, with a learned relative position bias, and what it learned is tied to the obstacle shapes of its training ground. It solves 51 % of 256 m routes in a synthetic house maze with thick walls (C5 report §4.9) but 4 % of a thin-wall maze, where the first pass already draws a round ring through the walls (C5 report §7). That failure is what the NCA literature would predict for a non-local rule.

**Transformers that imitate search traces.** Searchformer [16] trains an encoder-decoder transformer on token sequences of A* search dynamics on mazes and Sokoban; it outperforms models trained on optimal plans alone with a smaller model and dataset, and after bootstrapping it solves 93.7 % of unseen Sokoban puzzles optimally with up to 26.8 % fewer search steps than the A* that generated its data. Its stated limits are cost: traces can grow exponentially with plan length, most maze traces are thousands of tokens long, and Sokoban traces had to be capped at 10,000 tokens for GPU memory. Stream of Search [17] trains a language model on flattened search streams for the Countdown game and reports about 25 % higher search accuracy than training on optimal solutions, plus further gains from policy improvement. On the other side, Bachmann and Nagarajan [21] show a minimal planning task on which teacher-forced next-token training fails to learn the right predictor at all, and Nolte et al. [20] report that a multi-step-prediction objective beats next-token prediction on mazes, including next-token models given A* traces; Ivanitskiy et al. [18, 19] provide maze datasets and find that small maze-solving transformers form linearly decodable representations of the maze. The common thread is that a search serialised into tokens works but is long, and that the training objective decides whether planning is learned. Our wave search is the dense, visual version of the trace idea, and it sidesteps the length problem in a specific way: each pass rewrites the whole scratchpad in one forward pass, so the state never grows with route length; a 191 m route takes 11 passes (C5 report §1), and a pass takes 2.4 ms on an RTX 3060 (C5 report §4.5). The teacher is one deterministic wave per route, with no choices for the network to imitate, and that was the reason it was learnable where two earlier search targets with arbitrary choices were not (C5 report §1; `docs/memo/WAVE_SEARCH_2026-10-01.md`). It is not a better planner than A*: it paints much of the window on long routes, and classical path finding solves these maps exactly (C5 report §7).

**Exposure bias: training on the network's own outputs.** Imitated traces suffer from the gap between training on the teacher's states and running on one's own. Scheduled sampling [23] addresses this in sequence models by feeding the model's own previous tokens during training on a curriculum; DAgger [22] collects states visited by the learner, has the expert label them, and retrains, with no-regret guarantees. Our "natural noise" step is between the two: one checkpoint runs under the teacher's schedule over all 100k training routes, its drawing after every pass is stored (1.16 M passes), and training continues with half of the input drawings replaced by these stored ones, while the target stays the teacher's clean next drawing (C5 report §4.6). Unlike DAgger the target is not the expert's response to the corrupted state but the clean continuation, so the network learns to repair its input while advancing; unlike scheduled sampling the replacement is offline and in one round. The effect is large and confined to the loop: +11 to +16 points free-running (De Pijp val 64.0 → 77.6 %, test 61.0 → 72.8 %, never-seen district 44.2 → 59.6 %) with the teacher-forced scores unchanged, and training clean first and repairing afterwards beat mixing the stored drawings in from the start by 3–5 points at a third of the compute (C5 report §4.6). A related observation echoes the gap between teacher-forced and free-running behaviour reported in the trace literature: at two blocks the same recipe is a few points behind four blocks teacher-forced but solves 4 % free-running against 75 % (C5 report §4.3).

## 3. Scratchpads, chain of thought and iterative refinement

Language models solve multi-step problems better when they write intermediate steps: trained scratchpads [24] for long addition and program execution, chain-of-thought prompting [25] at scale, and scratchpad prompting as the main lever for length generalisation where naive fine-tuning fails regardless of model size [26]. Adaptive computation lets a network choose how many steps to take: Adaptive Computation Time [28] learns a halting signal for recurrent networks, the Universal Transformer [27] applies one block recurrently with per-position dynamic halting, and recurrent-depth language models [29] scale test-time compute by iterating a block in latent space. Test-time compute can also substitute for parameters on problems the base model partly solves [30]. In vision, iterative error feedback [31] refines a pose estimate over several passes by feeding back predicted corrections, Visual Sketchpad [32] gives multimodal LLMs a canvas and drawing tools as a visual chain of thought, and ViLaSR [33] trains vision-language models to reason by drawing boxes and auxiliary lines, with reinforcement learning.

The wave search sits at a particular point in this space. Its scratchpad is visual and dense, the whole output grid at 2 m cells, and it is not an annotation layered on a text reasoning process: it is the only state the computation has. The model's output is fed back verbatim as its next input, and nothing outside the network computes or selects anything (`PAPER.md` Section 5.1). The stop decision is a learned token, closer to ACT's halting than to a fixed iteration count, but discrete and supervised by the teacher's phase sequence rather than learned through a ponder cost. Two findings from this setting bear on the scratchpad literature. First, a scalar control head and a dense output head sharing one trunk need explicit balancing: at token-loss weight 1.0 the one cross-entropy per sample outweighed a grid loss averaged over 16,384 cells and blurred the drawing, and at 0.1 both the drawing and the token improved (C5 report §5.5). Second, the extra passes buy what one pass cannot: under the search's cell rule a one-shot network of the same size and total training budget solves 46 % of De Pijp validation routes against the search's 78 %, and the gap is smallest on the shortest routes and stays at 37–47 points beyond 4 fronts on the home district (narrowing on the mazes, where both methods fail on long routes) (C5 report §4.9). Unlike the language-model scratchpad, ours has no pretrained prior; the A1 experiment, the same interface on a Qwen3-VL backbone, is planned and not built (`PAPER.md` Section 8.7).

## 4. Position encodings, point prompts, shortcuts, attention sinks and task grouping

The one-shot ladder (`PAPER.md` Section 4) connects to four smaller literatures. Marker tasks only trained once the marker was drawn as a disc and attention had a learned bias per relative offset between patches, the relative position bias of the Swin Transformer [34]; with both, "within 100 m of the marker" trained in 1.5k steps instead of 6k (`PAPER.md` Sections 4.2 and 8.1; M1 report). Segment Anything [35] encodes a point prompt as a positional encoding of its location plus a learned embedding, a token rather than an input plane; we kept the marker as a plane, and the token route is untested here. "Food & drink within 100 m" sat at 0.41 IoU on a solution that ignores the marker, a shortcut in the sense of Geirhos et al. [36], and a cheap distance-profile probe distinguished it from the real solution (M1 report §2.1). Heads in the first block that sank onto the constant condition tokens resemble attention sinks [37], first described for the initial tokens of language models, and the high-norm artifact tokens that vision transformers repurpose for internal computation [38]; in our case the sink is a failure state in a tiny model that some runs recover from (C4 report §5, §7.2). Finally, the mix results are small data points for task grouping [39]: training the hard conjunction together with the plain distance task let it escape the shortcut in 10 of 11 runs, while training the straight segment with the detour hurt the detour by 0.03, two tasks with the same output form and contradicting rules competing for one circuit (`PAPER.md` Sections 4.2 and 8.3; C4 report §7.2).

## 5. What has been shown, what has not, and where this project sits

Prior work establishes that networks can learn to execute search and planning algorithms when the intermediate states are supervised [12, 13, 15, 16], that iteration buys generalisation to larger problems when the recurrence is local or re-reads the input [14, 15], that serialised search traces work but grow long [16], that teacher-forced training can miss what free-running needs [21, 23, 22], and that large vision-language models remain weak at route planning on maps [1, 5], with synthetic supervision giving modest gains [6]. This project adds a controlled, small-scale case that joins these threads: an 8.9 M-parameter transformer trained from scratch on aligned city layers runs a taught breadth-first search on its own visual scratchpad, with a learned state token and no code in the loop, and solves 76.4 % of 500 held-out De Pijp routes under a polyline rule shared with the baselines, against 49.6 % for Qwen3-VL-2B with LoRA on 19.3k routes and 37.4 % for a one-shot network at the same budget; on routes of six or more fronts the figures are 63 %, 28 % and 15 % (Comparison table §2). The failures differ in kind: the fine-tuned VLM fails by geometry, lines through buildings with the right ends, and the search fails by continuity, gaps in a drawing that stays on the street (Comparison table §3; C5 report §6).

The limits are as plain. It is one city, a single seed per search configuration, and a teacher-given algorithm, not a discovered one (C5 report §7). The input interfaces differ between the grid models (one-hot planes, a marker plane) and Qwen (a rendered image, coordinates out), so the comparison holds routes and scorer fixed, not modality (Comparison table §5). Success falls with route length everywhere, and at the 512 m window the search solves 42 % overall but 4 % of routes with 13 or more fronts (C5 report §4.7). The learned obstacle rule does not transfer to thin walls (C5 report §7). The cleanup pass trades success for clean drawings at 256 m and fails at 512 m (C5 report §4.8). The Deep Thinking test, train short and run longer by iterating more, has not been run, so the claim is that the network runs the search it was taught on its own outputs, not that it extrapolates beyond its training distribution.
